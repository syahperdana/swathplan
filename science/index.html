Science and background

The formulas SwathPlan uses, where they come from, and what they assume. Each statement taken from a standard, paper, dataset or datasheet carries a numbered footnote to the original source in the references. Methods without a footnote are SwathPlan's own, and the text says so. How to use the tools is in the user guide.

1 Conventions

  • Distances in metres, speeds in knots on screen (1 kn = 0.5144 m/s in the formulas), angles from the vertical for beams and from north for azimuths.
  • d is the depth below the transducer, θ a beam angle, c the sound speed. Where one value is needed, c = 1500 m/s, the usual nominal value for seawater3.
  • Uncertainties are given at the 95 % confidence level, as in IHO S-44: 1.96σ for one-dimensional (vertical) quantities and 2.45σ for two-dimensional (horizontal) ones1.
  • Unless stated, the seabed under each swath or footprint is taken as flat at the depth under the line. This is the usual planning approximation; real slopes widen or narrow the swath.

2 IHO S-44, the survey standard

SwathPlan checks plans against IHO S-44 Standards for Hydrographic Surveys, Edition 6.2.0 (October 2024)1, adopted unanimously by the IHO Member States in April 20252. The orders and their requirements (S-44 Table 1) are:

The maximum allowed total vertical uncertainty at depth d is1

TVU_max(d) = √( a² + (b · d)² )

For example, Exclusive Order at 25 m gives √(0.15² + (0.0075 × 25)²) = 0.24 m.

Cross-line check. Where a cross line meets a main line, the same seabed is measured twice. If both soundings meet TVU_max independently, their difference has a standard deviation √2 times larger, so at 95 % the difference should stay within about √2 · TVU_max(d). The results show this value for the depth range; it follows from the propagation of independent errors and is a guide, not an S-44 rule.

3 Multibeam line planning

3.1 Swath width and line spacing

A multibeam echosounder ensonifies a fan of beams across the track3. On a flat seabed at depth d, with the outer beams at θp (port) and θs (starboard) from the vertical, the swath width is

W = d · ( tan θp + tan θs )

With overlap fraction o (the overlap width divided by the swath width), neighbouring lines are

S = W · ( 1 − o )                e.g. d = 25 m, ±60°, o = 30 %: W = 86.6 m, S = 60.6 m

Overlap backs up the noisy outer beams with good inner beams of the next line and absorbs steering and depth errors. Because W grows with depth, the shallowest depth in the area sets the spacing that gives full coverage everywhere; the 5th percentile is a common compromise.

The planner asks for the seabed coverage C rather than the overlap: 100 % means neighbouring swaths just meet and 200 % that every point of the seabed is in two swaths. The two are related by C = 100 % / (1 − o), so 50 % overlap is 200 % coverage and 80 % overlap is 500 %.

3.2 Adaptive line spacing (SwathPlan's method)

With depth data loaded, SwathPlan places each line as far from the previous one as the local depth allows. For consecutive lines at across-track positions s and s′, with the half-swaths facing each other h(t) and h′(t) computed from the depth under each line along the line coordinate t, the condition is

s′ − s ≤ min over t of [ h(t) + h′(t) − o · ( W(t) + W′(t) ) / 2 ]

The largest s′ that meets it is found by bisection. Lines alternate direction, so port and starboard angles swap from one line to the next, and the code accounts for this. The first and last lines are placed so that the swath reaches the boundary. Like the uniform case, it assumes a flat seabed under each swath at the depth under the line.

3.3 Contour-following lines (SwathPlan's method)

With Follow a depth contour on, the main lines run parallel to one isobath instead of being straight. On a sloping seabed a line along an isobath keeps an even depth, so its swath width stays even.

  1. The depth is sampled on a regular grid over the area (about 300 cells across) and the isobath at the chosen depth is traced with marching squares34. Saddle cells are resolved with the mean of the four corners. Where several pieces exist, the one with the most length inside the area is used.
  2. The isobath is smoothed with two passes of Chaikin's corner cutting35, simplified with the Douglas–Peucker algorithm36, and extended straight at both ends so that it spans the area.
  3. A signed distance to this base line (positive to starboard) is computed on the grid, and every line is the level set at k · S, again traced with marching squares, for whole numbers k. Level sets of a distance field are the exact offset curves without the loops that offsetting a curve point by point produces on its concave side, so neighbouring lines never cross and stay one spacing S apart.
  4. Each line is cut to the area and, with trimming on, to water outside the safety clearance; the cut ends are refined by bisection. Pieces shorter than the smaller of 50 m and S are dropped.
  5. Lines are run row by row in alternating directions. Between rows the turn is built as in section 8.1, from the heading at the end of the line; along a row, a straight transit joins the pieces.

The spacing is the design spacing S of section 3.1 at the design depth: only the line on the base isobath has exactly that depth, and the others are offset curves, not isobaths. Choose the base depth near the shallow side of the area so that the spacing holds where the swath is narrowest. Adaptive spacing (3.2) is not applied.

3.4 Ping rate, along-track sampling and speed

Each ping must travel to the farthest point of the swath and back before the next, so the highest ping rate is3

f_max = c / (2 R),     R = d / cos θmax         d = 25 m, 60°: R = 50 m, f_max = 15 Hz

At speed v the pings are Δx = v / f apart along the track, and the along-track footprint at nadir is about d · β (β = along-track beamwidth in radians)3. To make sure a small feature is hit by several pings, SwathPlan uses the rule that NOAA set for side-scan sonar: an object must be ensonified at least three times per pass, which gives a maximum speed4

v_max = f · T / 3          (T = smallest feature size of the chosen S-44 order)

Applied to multibeam it is a planning guide, not an S-44 requirement.

4 Uncertainty budget (a priori TPU)

The total propagated uncertainty of a sounding combines many independent error sources: range and beam angle, sound speed, vessel motion, heading, positioning, water level and draft5. SwathPlan uses a simplified a priori model after Hare (1995)5, with σ for the 1σ input of each source, all angles in radians, x = d tan θ the across-track distance and l = d / cos θ the slant range:

σα  = √( σroll² + σpointing² )          beam angle
Δθ  = tan θ · σc0 / c                  refraction from the sound speed at the transducer

σz² = (σR cos θ)² + (d tan θ σα)² + σheave² + σtide² + σdraft²
      + (d σc / c)² + (d tan θ Δθ)²
σh² = σpos² + (x σhdg)² + (d σα)² + (l σpitch)²
      + (x σc / c)² + (d Δθ)² + (σR sin θ)² + σdraft²

TVU = 1.96 σz        THU = 2.45 σh

Here σc0 is the uncertainty of the sound speed at the transducer and σc that of the mean sound-speed profile. TVU and THU are computed for every beam angle from nadir to the sensor's maximum, at the design depth, and compared with the S-44 limits of section 21. The widest angle that still meets the order is offered as the swath angle. The model leaves out cross-terms, time latency, lever-arm errors and the sonar's own bottom-detection uncertainty, which grows at the outer beams3, so check it against your system's own uncertainty model.

5 Single-beam surveys

A single-beam transducer with full beam width β ensonifies a footprint of diameter about

w = 2 d tan(β / 2)

on a flat seabed. The main lines alone cover the fraction w / S of the area, and SwathPlan estimates the bathymetric coverage as (Σ main line length + Σ cross line length) · w / area. S-44 accepts less than full coverage only for Order 2 and Order 1b. These need at least 5 % bathymetric coverage, and S-44 6.2.0 asks that registered depth positions be no more than 3 times the water depth, or 25 m, apart, whichever is greater (sections 1.1 and 3.5.2)1. Use the S-44 maximum applies this rule at the design depth. Order 1a needs a 100 % feature search, and Special and Exclusive Order need full bathymetric coverage, which a single beam cannot give1.

The transducer presets are typical beam widths for 200 kHz and 33 kHz survey transducers, not values of a particular model.

6 Geophysical surveys

6.1 Side-scan sonar

A side-scan sonar images a strip on each side of the towfish out to its range R, with a gap under the towfish about as wide as its height above the seabed on each side6. For a seabed coverage C (100 % = every point seen once, 200 % = twice4) and an overlap margin m:

S = 2R / (C / 100) · (1 − m)

200 % coverage, with alternate lines filling each other's nadir gap, is the usual practice for object detection46. The ping rate is limited by the two-way travel to the range, f = c / (2R), and the speed by the three-ping rule4:

v_max = L_target · f / n_pings          (NOAA: 1 m object, 3 pings)

The model and frequency list gives each system's longest range per side from its datasheet: EdgeTech 4125 (150 m at 400 kHz, 75 m at 900 kHz)22, EdgeTech 4200 (230 m at 300 kHz, 120 m at 600 kHz)23, Klein 3900 (150 m at 445 kHz, 50 m at 900 kHz)24 and Klein 4900 (200 m at 455 kHz, 75 m at 900 kHz)25; section 14 lists the other models and their sources. These are maximum operating ranges. The usable range in a given survey also depends on the towfish height, the water conditions and the target size, so the range field stays editable.

6.2 Magnetometer

The anomaly of a ferrous object falls off quickly with distance (with the cube of the distance for a dipole)7. So the planner asks for the distance D at which your target can still be detected, and the sensor height h above the seabed. A seabed point is within reach if it is within D of the sensor, so the lines can be up to

S = 2 √( D² − h² )              (only when D > h)

apart. At speed v and sample rate f, samples are v / f apart along the line. Choosing D for a target is a matter of the target's magnetic moment and the site's noise. The planner does not model it.

6.3 Sub-bottom profiler and UHR seismic

Both use the grid spacing you enter. For 2D seismic, a line only reaches full fold once the whole streamer has passed over the start of the target area8, so the planner extends seismic lines by half the active streamer length at each end. Towed sensors add their layback behind the antenna to the run-in. The spacing used is the smallest of the ticked sensors.

7 Drone photogrammetry and LiDAR

For a nadir camera with sensor width Sw and height Sh (mm), focal length F (mm, the real focal length, not the 35 mm equivalent), image width N pixels, at height H above ground910:

footprint across = H · Sw / F       footprint along = H · Sh / F
GSD = footprint across / N           (ground sample distance, m per pixel)
line spacing     = footprint across · (1 − side overlap)
photo distance   = footprint along  · (1 − forward overlap)
photo interval   = photo distance / v

For example, the DJI Mavic 3 Enterprise wide camera (17.3 × 13 mm, 12.3 mm, 5280 px)26 at 100 m gives a 140.7 m footprint across, a GSD of 2.66 cm, 49.2 m line spacing at 65 % side overlap and a photo every 26.4 m at 75 % forward overlap. The defaults, 75 % forward and 65 % side, follow common practice: Pix4D recommends at least 75 % forward and 60 % side for general mapping10. The plan check compares the photo interval with the camera's shortest interval: 0.7 s for the Mavic 3 Enterprise26 and the Zenmuse P129, 0.5 s for the Matrice 4E27 and 2.5 s for the Phantom 4 RTK28.

Photos are counted as Σ line length / photo distance plus one per line. Batteries are the flight time divided by the time per battery, rounded up, and each change adds its time to the plan. The height warning uses 120 m, the limit for the EU open category11; the US limit is 400 ft above ground level12. Local rules and permissions decide.

The waypoint exports switch distance-based triggering on after the first waypoint with MAVLink command 206, MAV_CMD_DO_SET_CAM_TRIGG_DIST (parameter 1 the distance, parameter 3 one picture at once), and off at the end with a distance of 013.

LiDAR. A scanner with across-track field of view FOV, point rate P (points per second, single return), at height H and ground speed v, over flat ground:

swath          = 2 · H · tan(FOV / 2)
line spacing   = swath · (1 − side overlap)
density, 1 pass = P / (v · swath)                (points per m²)
density        = density, 1 pass / (1 − side overlap)
slant range at the swath edge = H / cos(FOV / 2)
highest speed for a target density D = P / (D · (1 − side overlap) · swath)

The average density counts every emitted point once on the ground; with side overlap each point of the ground is swept 1 / (1 − overlap) times on average. The detection range is the maker's figure for 10 % reflectivity in bright light, which is about the darkest common ground (asphalt, wet soil). The plan check compares it with the height and with the slant range at the swath edge. For example, the DJI Zenmuse L2 (70° across, 240,000 points/s single return, 250 m at 10 % reflectivity and 100 klx)38 at 100 m and 10 m/s sweeps 140 m and gives 171 points/m² per pass, 343 with 50 % side overlap; the edge slant range is 122 m. The L1 has a 70.4° field of view in repetitive scanning, the same 240,000 points/s single return and 190 m at 10 %39. The L3 scans up to 80°, its pulse rate is adjustable from 100 kHz to 2 MHz, and it detects 700 m at 10 % reflectivity at 350 kHz (950 m at 100 kHz)40. In non-repetitive scanning the pattern also spreads along track, but the average density per pass is the same, since it depends only on the point rate and the area swept per second.

7.1 Terrain following (SwathPlan's method)

Over sloping ground a fixed flight altitude changes the height above ground, and with it the image scale, the footprint, the overlaps and the LiDAR swath and density. With terrain following, the planner samples a terrain model (SRTM 30 m from the AWS Terrain Tiles6432, the NOAA NCEI DEM mosaic21, or a GeoTIFF or ESRI ASCII elevation grid) on a regular grid over the plan and interpolates it bilinearly. Each line is split into waypoints every d metres (the waypoint spacing). With z(·) the terrain, z₀ the ground at the take-off point and H the flight height:

leg maximum   m_k = max z along the leg from waypoint k to k+1   (sampled every min(10 m, d/4))
height of waypoint k above take-off = H + max(m_k−1, m_k) − z₀

Both ends of every leg are then at least H above the highest sampled ground on that leg, and so is every point of the straight leg between them. A waypoint is dropped when the straight leg from the last kept waypoint to the next one passes between 0.5 m below and 2 m above it, so waypoints remain only where the slope changes; line ends are always kept. The plan check reports the range of heights above ground along the lines, the resulting range of GSD (camera) or the slant range at the highest point (LiDAR), and warns above 700 waypoints, the mission size of many ArduPilot boards. Without terrain following, a loaded model is used to warn where the ground rises above the flight altitude or where the height above ground changes by more than 20 %. The exports give each waypoint its height relative to the take-off point (MAVLink frame 3, MAV_FRAME_GLOBAL_RELATIVE_ALT)13.

A terrain model gives bare-earth or surface heights at its own resolution and accuracy: SRTM was specified to 16 m absolute height error at 90 % confidence, and it smooths away objects smaller than its 30 m cells64. Trees, buildings, masts and wires are not in the plan. The margin is the pilot's.

8 Turns, time and cost

8.1 Williamson-style and U-turns

The vessel runs the next line in the opposite direction. With line spacing S and turning radius R:

  • If S ≥ 2R, a U-turn: two quarter circles and a straight connector of length S − 2R.
  • If S < 2R, a bulb turn named after the Williamson man-overboard manoeuvre: turn away from the next line by an angle α, then the opposite way by 180° + α, ending on the reciprocal heading on the next line.
S = 2R cos α  →  α = arccos( S / 2R )
path length = Rα + R(π + α) = R(π + 2α)
overshoot beyond the line end = 2R sin α          R = 35 m, S = 60.6 m: α = 30°, 146.6 m, 35 m

This is geometry with a constant radius. Real vessels have a limited rate of turn and slow down in turns, so enter a measured radius. Recommend R from LOA uses 2.5 × the vessel length as a first guess, a rule of thumb of SwathPlan, not a published value.

8.2 Time and cost (SwathPlan's model)

on-line time   = Σ line lengths / v
turn time      = Σ turn and transit lengths / v_turn
productive     = on-line + turns
total project  = (productive + SVP casts + calibration) / (1 − w) + mobilisation
cost           = total project (days) × day rate

Here w is the weather and downtime allowance. SVP time is the number of casts (productive time divided by the cast interval) times the cast duration. Days are the total divided by the operating hours per day. On-line efficiency is on-line time over productive time; a low value means turns dominate. Pings are the ping rate times the on-line time, and soundings are pings times beams. For drones, battery changes take the place of SVP casts and there is no slow-down in turns.

9 Corridors and segments (SwathPlan's method)

Corridor lines are offsets of the route centreline at fixed distances (starboard positive), cut to the route ends and extended for run-in and run-out. KP is the distance along the route from its first point plus the starting KP. A route of length L is split into n = ⌈L / Lseg⌉ segments, and segment i (from 0) runs from

max(0, i·Lseg − O/2)   to   min(L, (i+1)·Lseg + O/2)

so neighbouring segments overlap by O. Each segment gets its own offset lines from the cut piece of the route and its own outline; between segments the vessel transits in a straight line. When exporting one file per segment, a cross line goes to the first segment whose outline holds its middle.

10 Coordinates and datums

The map works in a local metric frame around the area's centre (an equirectangular approximation, accurate for areas of some tens of kilometres). Conversion to UTM or a custom Transverse Mercator, and back, uses the series formulas of Snyder (1987)14. Datum shifts use the abridged Molodensky transformation with three translation parameters15, accurate to a few metres with published parameters. EPSG codes are converted with the proj4js library16; datum grid files are not supported.

11 Coastlines and land avoidance

OpenStreetMap coastlines are drawn with the land on the left and the water on the right of the line17. SwathPlan intersects each survey line with the coastline, cuts it at the crossings, decides which side is water from the nearest segment, and removes a clearance zone, a capsule with the radius of the safety distance around each coastline segment. Land polygons use an inside/outside test (even-odd) instead, which does not depend on the direction of the rings.

The built-in coastline is Natural Earth, a public-domain dataset18. For land avoidance SwathPlan uses the 1:10m land polygons with minor islands, cut to the area. The 1:10m coastline lines are not used for this: their direction is not consistent, and most continental coasts are open pieces, so the land side cannot be told from them. The 1:50m coastline, kept inside each page as a fallback, has closed rings stored in both directions, so SwathPlan turns every closed ring counter-clockwise (land on the left) before use. Natural Earth is generalised for small-scale maps: expect errors of a few hundred metres at 1:10m and about a kilometre at 1:50m. For detailed work, load GSHHG (full resolution)19 or OpenStreetMap land polygons as GeoJSON.

Turn paths are checked against the same coastline. Each turn is a polyline of points along its arcs. A turn is flagged when any of its straight pieces crosses a coastline segment (a proper intersection, found with orientation tests) or comes closer to one than the safety clearance (the shortest segment-to-segment distance, the least of the four point-to-segment distances), or when its middle point lies on land, which catches a turn wholly inside a land polygon. A grid of the coastline segments, with cells of at least 200 m and four times the clearance, keeps the test fast for thousands of turns.

13 Infill planning

The infill planner rasterises the area into cells (the grid cell size). From a processed GeoTIFF33, a cell is covered when the grid or mosaic has data there. From track lines, a cell is covered when it falls inside a track's swath. For multibeam the swath comes from the depth and swath angles; for side-scan it runs from the nadir gap to the range on each side6. At 200 % coverage a cell counts as covered only when two tracks see it4. Uncovered cells are grouped into gaps, gaps smaller than the chosen minimum are ignored, and lines are planned across each gap with the spacing of the chosen coverage. Each line is cut into runs where its strip has gap cells; a covered stretch longer than four cells (or twice the line extension) splits it. With a margin outside the boundary, cells within that distance of an edge or a corner are added, each assigned to its edge. Margin gaps are grouped per edge and planned along it, and gaps are visited nearest first. The coverage percentage counts only the cells inside the boundary.

15 Assumptions and limitations

  • Flat seabed under each swath or footprint; no refraction or beam-angle limits other than those you enter.
  • A single nominal sound speed (1500 m/s) for ping rates.
  • Straight, parallel lines for areas, or offsets of one isobath when following a contour; offset lines for corridors.
  • Constant speeds; currents, traffic and port calls are not modelled.
  • Turns are checked against the loaded coastline only; shoals, structures and traffic are not considered.
  • Coastlines and global bathymetry are not navigation data. Plan against official charts and local notices.
